What the model learns about epistasis, and how to measure it

A review of the epistasis hierarchy, of the two things that make doubles look non-additive without any specific interaction (the assay's saturation and nonspecific epistasis), of the mechanisms that are meant to learn each level, and of the validation metrics with the evidence behind each one. Every number comes from the Tsuboyama data (the capped split's validation libraries, or every library where noted), from validation dumps of past runs, or from simulation on the real designs.

Summary

Three kinds

Measured dddG mixes three things. Readout saturation: the assay cannot report a dG much below a floor near 0 or above 5, and it compresses every variant near the floor, singles included. Nonspecific epistasis: departures from additivity that depend on the substitutions only through the sizes of their effects, so they appear in multi-mutants only. Specific epistasis: what depends on which residues at which positions. The hierarchy's global level, E[dddG | x], holds most of the first two.

Variance

56 to 59% of the variance of validation dddG is a function of the additive expectation x alone. A saturation curve built from the singles' own evidence reproduces about three quarters of that level. The rest is nonspecific epistasis: in range, dddG falls with x by about 0.6 to 0.7 kcal/mol in both protease readouts, even where every variant reads far above the floor. Its level (+0.3 to +0.5 in the combined readout) is uncertain by about ±0.2, because the two readouts disagree about the wild-type reference. Another 6% depends on the two single effects beyond x, mostly through the compression of near-floor singles, and comes out of the pair level (23 to 17%). Line effects hold 12%, the specific combination 6%.

Telling them apart

Saturation belongs to the readout: it acts on singles and doubles alike through one monotone curve, and it differs between the two proteases (practical floors −0.44 and −0.02). Nonspecific epistasis acts on multi-mutants only and is the same in both readouts. Five tests use these properties: doubles far above the floor (selected in one protease, scored in the other, so noise cannot bias them), the two readouts, a saturation curve built from the singles, the lift test, and a monotone fit that can absorb nonspecific epistasis only by turning degenerate. The in-range decline is settled; the knee (x from −1 to 0) is still confounded.

The link

A monotone link can model saturation, not nonspecific epistasis. Its learned floor has not been where the data put it. In devel a censoring hinge at the nominal bound −1 drags it down and softens it through training (−0.9 to −1.2, τ 0.54 to 1.1). The released objective has no hinge and regresses measured doubles through it, and settles near −0.5 within a few hundred steps. Every trained model under-predicts the global curve by 0.5 to 0.7 kcal/mol, which is mostly the nonspecific hump; the MT head absorbs part of it.

Metrics

The 17 epistasis metrics (logged for every head since 2026-10-13) remove E[· | x] whatever causes it, so the hump never reaches the residual or rank levels. Nonspecific epistasis that depends on the two single effects beyond x does: in simulation a predictor that knows only a product-form term earns pair_rho 0.31 and reversal accuracy 0.57. A two-stage device that also removes a smooth surface in the two single effects (fitted with the library held out) brings that to 0 and keeps the credit for specific effects. On the real models it lowers pair_rho by 0.08 to 0.14. cell_rank is immune to every nonspecific form tested.

Stop on

val_epi_cell_rank_mt, confirmed by cell_mag_comb and cell_flipacc_mt (replicate noise 0.005, 0.007 and 0.010). Read reversal accuracy as secondary: nonspecific epistasis of the product kind can earn it.

Runs so far

The released objective (teacher-forced, MT rank 16) still leads every devel arm at the cell level at matched epochs; 23 devel arms (MT rank, the reversal loss, whole-matrix packing, floor censoring, MT component weights) did not close the gap. With the link it reproduces the global curve best of all models (curve RMSE 0.68 at epoch 2, against 0.88 without).

Objectives

Keep the link a mild saturation model with no hinge at −1, teacher-force with latent rather than measured singles, and give nonspecific epistasis its own small term for multi-mutants. Simulation (Table 8) shows what such a term can and cannot recover from realistic single-mutant predictions.

The hierarchy and its variance

A double mutant carries residue a at position i and b at j (i < j) in a library whose wild type has stability c = dG_wt. It has a measured ddG_AB, both measured singles, and the epistasis score dddG = ddG_AB − ddG_A − ddG_B. Its additive expectation x = c + ddG_A + ddG_B is the dG the double would have if the substitutions did not interact. All doubles of one position pair form a matrix, rows a and columns b (19 × 19 when complete; the 24 validation matrices are 324 to 361 cells), so dddG divides into four nested levels:

The levels describe where a departure from additivity sits, not what causes it. Three causes matter here. Readout saturation is the assay's: a monotone response that compresses every variant near its floor or ceiling. Nonspecific epistasis is in the stability itself but depends on the two substitutions only through the sizes of their effects (and the stability they act at): two destabilising substitutions that add less than their sum, whatever they are. Specific epistasis depends on which residues meet at which positions. The global level collects whatever is a function of x alone, saturation and nonspecific epistasis both. Nonspecific epistasis that depends on the two effects separately (for example on how unequal they are) is not a function of x, and lands in the pair and line levels.

The levels are additive in variance when each is computed on what the level above leaves. On the validation doubles (in-sample, complete blocks), and in the earlier training-set ANOVA (127,476 doubles, cross-validated so noise fitted by row and column means is not counted as explained):

Variance along the additive expectation

The partition changes with x. Figure 2b fits the hierarchy once over every library's doubles (133,647 with a numeric dddG) and then reads each component's variance inside five bins of x, beside the mean dddG split into readout saturation (the saturation curve built from the singles) and the nonspecific remainder, and beside a noise floor for the cell level: the measurement noise of the double's own dG, estimated from the disagreement of the two protease readouts (a robust variance of dG_t − dG_c, divided by 4).

  • Below the floor (x < −1) the mean dddG (2.04) is nearly all saturation (1.95), and most of the variance is the global curve's own slope (0.27 of 0.64 kcal²). The cell variance (0.077) is below the double's noise (0.131): there is no combination-specific signal to learn.
  • The knee (−1 to 0) splits its mean between saturation (0.61) and nonspecific epistasis (0.42); the cell variance equals the noise floor.
  • In range (x > 0) saturation is gone (0.11, then about 0), the nonspecific part carries the mean (0.47, 0.36, 0.10), and the cell variance rises above the noise: 0.012 kcal² above it at x = 0 to 1, 0.022 at 1 to 2 and 0.037 above 2, about a quarter, two fifths and three fifths of the cell level. The specific interaction the cell metrics target lives in the in-range doubles.
  • Pair and line levels are about constant along x (0.10 to 0.13 and 0.12 to 0.16 kcal²), as are the nonspecific effects beyond x (0.03): they are not saturation phenomena. The line level includes the singles' own noise, which every cell of a line shares.

What the noise leaves to learn

Measurement noise is about 0.2 to 0.27 kcal/mol per variant (replicate rows of the same mutant give 0.27; protease disagreement 0.22 to 0.31). Three facts follow, and they set the ceiling for every metric below:

  • The cell level is at the noise floor. A double's own noise, double-centred over a 19 × 19 block, contributes 0.9σ², which is 0.036 to 0.066. The measured cell variance is 0.040. The specific interaction is therefore a small share of the variance (about 0 to 5%, most likely about 2%, in the training ANOVA). It is still real: trained models get the direction of 80 to 88% of confident order reversals right, and the measured reversals are far more frequent than noise alone produces.
  • Most of the line level is the singles' noise. dddG subtracts the measured ddG_A from every cell of A's line, so that single's error becomes a line effect of variance σ² (0.04 to 0.07). The measured line variance is 0.037 (rows) and 0.053 (columns). A model cannot predict this part on new proteins, so line metrics are attenuated by it, not biased.
  • The pair level is mostly library-level, and partly not epistasis. 70% of the variance of the 24 pair offsets is between the 9 libraries, so the pair level has about 9 independent units in validation. Much of the between-library part is the readout: dddG adds the library's wild-type dG, so an error in that reference shifts every double of the library. Across 116 libraries the library mean of in-range dddG differs between the two protease readouts with an SD of 0.38 kcal/mol, as large as the spread of the library means themselves (0.39), and the difference follows the two readouts' wild-type values (r 0.33). About a quarter of the remaining pair level is nonspecific epistasis beyond x (Figure 2). The pair level can be reported, not optimised.

Saturation and nonspecific epistasis

What the assay can and cannot see

Every number here is a stability, dG in kcal/mol: how strongly the folded state is favoured over the unfolded one. Higher means more stable. A substitution's ddG is the change it makes to dG (negative = destabilising). The assay (cDNA display proteolysis) does not measure dG directly. It measures how much protease a protein survives and converts that into a dG, and the conversion has two limits.

  • The floor. A protein that is unfolded anyway is cut whether it is a little unstable or very unstable, so every unfolded variant reads about the same value. In practice the floor sits between about −0.5 and +0.5 kcal/mol, depending on the protein and the protease (the nominal reporting limit is −1).
  • The ceiling. An extremely stable protein survives everything, so readings stop rising near 5.

Between the two the reading tracks the true stability. Near the floor it bends: a variant truly at −2 and one truly at −0.5 both read about 0.

Call the true stability the latent dG, and the curve that turns it into a reading the readout curve h. Figure S1 puts a wild type, two single mutants and their double on that curve. Set both kinds of epistasis to zero and the two substitutions do not interact at all. Even so, the measured dddG is not zero whenever one of the four variants sits on the bend. That is readout saturation. The presets show the cases this report keeps returning to.

The formula

Write each variant's latent stability, what the assay would report if it had no limits, as c for the wild type, c + a and c + b for the singles and c + a + b + n + e for the double. Here n is nonspecific epistasis (a function of a, b and c only) and e is specific epistasis. The assay reports h(latent) plus noise, with h monotone and saturating. Each measured ddG is h(c + effect) − h(c), so

dddG = ddG_AB − ddG_A − ddG_B = h(c+a+b+n+e) − h(c+a) − h(c+b) + h(c) = [ h(c+a+b) − h(c+a) − h(c+b) + h(c) ] + [ h(c+a+b+n+e) − h(c+a+b) ] + noise readout saturation S: the singles only epistasis through the local slope ≈ h′(c+a+b) · (n + e)

The split is exact (add and subtract h(c+a+b)); only the linearisation of the second bracket is approximate. It holds for the measurements under one assumption, that every variant of a library is read through the same h. Neither bracket can be computed from one double, because a, b, c, n and e are latent; the metrics rely on two devices that remove the first bracket exactly (below). The two kinds of global epistasis behave differently. S belongs to the readout: it needs no parameter beyond h, the singles' own readings fix it, and a second readout of the same proteins would change it. n belongs to the stability: it applies to multi-mutants only, and two readouts see the same n. Both are largest where the double is destabilised, so on a plot of dddG against x they overlap.

Telling them apart

Figure 3 shows the difficulty. Plotted against the additive expectation x, mean dddG rises steadily as x falls, and both causes predict exactly that.

  • Saturation does, because the more destabilised the double, the more of its loss the floor hides (Figure S1, "double below the floor").
  • Nonspecific epistasis does too, on the usual biophysical account: the more destabilised a protein already is, the less one more destabilising substitution costs it.

A single curve cannot say which cause made it. What can is a property that one cause has and the other lacks. There are four:

Readout saturation
  • A property of the assay, not of the protein.
  • Acts on every variant through the same curve: wild type, singles and doubles alike.
  • Tied to where the floor is. A second readout with a different floor saturates differently.
  • Acts only near the floor (or the ceiling). Between them the readout is a straight line, and a straight line cannot create any dddG.
Nonspecific epistasis
  • A property of the protein's stability.
  • Acts on multi-mutants only: a single cannot interact with itself.
  • The same in any readout of the same proteins.
  • Can act anywhere, including far above the floor.

Each of the five tests below uses one of these differences. For each: the question, what each cause predicts, what the data show, and how much weight the answer can bear. The first two tests rely on a detail of the data. Every variant was measured twice, once with trypsin and once with chymotrypsin, and each protease gives its own dG estimate. The two estimates have independent measurement noise and different floors (−0.44 and −0.02 kcal/mol, medians over all libraries).

Test 1. Doubles far above the floor

Does dddG still change with x when no variant is anywhere near the floor?

If saturation: no. Where all four variants read well above the floor, the readout is straight, and a straight readout passes additivity through unchanged. dddG would be about 0 at every x.
If nonspecific epistasis: yes. It does not need the floor, so the decline with x continues.

How. Keep the doubles whose wild type and both singles read at least 2 kcal/mol and whose additive expectation is at least 1, so the double itself is also expected well above the floor. There is one trap: choosing doubles by their measured values can manufacture a pattern out of noise. A single that happened to read too high is more likely to pass the cut. dddG subtracts that single, so its error then shows up in that double's dddG with the opposite sign. To avoid this, select on one protease's readings and compute dddG from the other protease's readings, whose noise has nothing to do with the selection.

Result (Figure 3c, left). dddG falls steadily with x whichever protease is scored:

  • scored in chymotrypsin, from 0.81 at x ≈ 1.3 to 0.13 at x ≈ 3.2;
  • scored in trypsin, from 0.27 to −0.43.

The selected doubles really are far above the floor: their median reading is 2.6 kcal/mol in chymotrypsin and 2.2 in trypsin, and 95% read above 1.3 and 1.0.

Could it be the ceiling instead? Near 5 the readout bends the other way, which pushes dddG negative at high x. Restricting to libraries whose wild type reads at most 3 kcal/mol, far below 5, leaves the decline in place (Figure 3c, right).

Verdict. In range, the way dddG depends on x is not floor saturation and not ceiling saturation. This is the strongest of the five tests.

Test 2. Two readouts of the same proteins

Does the curve move when the floor moves?

If saturation: the two proteases' curves differ near the floor, because their floors are 0.4 kcal/mol apart, and both are flat in range.
If nonspecific epistasis: the two curves have the same shape, in range included.

How. Compute each protease's own dddG and average it by x. The noise trap appears here in another form. x adds the measured singles and dddG subtracts them. A single that reads too low therefore moves its doubles left on the x axis and up in dddG. So binning dddG by an x built from the same readings tilts the curve downward even with no epistasis at all. Taking x from one protease and dddG from the other removes the tilt (Figure 3d, solid lines).

Result. In range the two proteases' curves fall in parallel, by about 0.7 kcal/mol between x ≈ 0.25 and 3.2. The noise tilt is real but modest: binning by the same protease's x (dashed lines) steepens the trypsin curve by about 0.15 over that span and leaves chymotrypsin's slope about unchanged. Toward the floor the curves draw apart a little, as their different floors predict.

The two curves are also offset by about 0.4 to 0.5 kcal/mol at every x. That offset is not epistasis. dddG contains the wild type's reading (dddG = dG_AB − dG_A − dG_B + dG_wt), so an error in a library's wild-type reading shifts every double of that library by the same amount, and the two proteases disagree about the wild types.

Verdict. The in-range slope belongs to the proteins. Its level has no fixed zero. At x ≈ 1.5 to 2, chymotrypsin puts doubles about 0.35 kcal/mol above additive and trypsin about 0, with the combined readout in between. The slope is settled. The level is uncertain by about ±0.2.

Test 3. How much can saturation explain, built from the singles?

If the floor alone made the curve, how large would the curve be?

How. Build the saturation that the data themselves imply, one step at a time:

  1. Find each library's floor: the median reading of its doubles that are expected far below it (x < −1.5).
  2. Assume a readout curve that bends at that floor, with softness τ (how gradual the bend is).
  3. Take every measured single back through the curve to its latent value. A single that reads just above the floor may truly be deeper. The step is capped at 0.5 kcal/mol, the size the lift test (Test 4) supports.
  4. Add the latent singles to get the latent double, push it through the curve, and compute the dddG this pure-saturation world would show.
  5. Average by x and compare with the measured curve.

Result (Figure 3b, right). Below the floor this reproduces the measured curve almost exactly. In range it gives almost nothing. It leaves +0.3 to +0.5 kcal/mol from x ≈ −0.9 to 1.4, falling to 0.18 at x ≈ 2.4. Over the whole curve it accounts for 67 to 84% of the global level's variance, depending on τ (73% at τ 0.4). In simulation, the same procedure recovers about three quarters of a planted nonspecific term and reads 0.06 when there is none.

Sensitivity. The table gives the remainder at several x for three softnesses. In range it hardly depends on τ. At the knee (x −1 to 0) it does: a softer bend lets saturation reach further up and leaves less for nonspecific epistasis.

Test 4. The lift test

How much does the floor compress the singles?

How. A single that reads 0.3 might truly sit at 0.3, or truly at −1.5 and be squashed onto the floor. Pair it with a stabilising partner that lifts the double back onto the straight part of the readout. The double is then read correctly, but the additive expectation still uses the single's compressed reading. If the single was compressed, the double lands below its additive expectation by the hidden amount (Figure S1, preset "lift test"). The test compares doubles of near-floor singles (read at dG 0 to 0.5) with doubles of comfortably folded singles (1.5 to 2.5), both with a stabilising partner.

If saturation: negative, because the near-floor singles are deeper than they read.
If nonspecific epistasis of the simulated kind: positive, because these doubles sit at an x where it pushes dddG up.

Result. Doubles of near-floor singles land 0.53 kcal/mol below their additive expectation, against 0.13 for the reference, a difference of −0.40. The near-floor singles are about 0.4 kcal/mol deeper than they read, which is why Test 3 caps the step at 0.5.

An unresolved tension. In simulation, saturation alone gives −0.37, and saturation plus the simulated nonspecific term gives +0.09. The real −0.40 looks like saturation alone. There are two possible readings:

  • Near-floor singles are compressed more than the simulated floor compresses them. Then saturation explains more of the knee than Test 3 says.
  • Nonspecific epistasis is not a function of x alone. A product-form term, for example, gives −0.27.

Either way the knee, x from −1 to 0, is where the two causes cannot yet be separated.

Test 5. Let a flexible readout explain everything

Can one readout curve, with no nonspecific term, explain the data?

How. Fit one monotone curve (always rising, otherwise any shape) to every double, pass the singles through the same curve, and see what shape it has to take.

Result. It can reproduce the global curve, to within 0.16 kcal/mol, but only by becoming absurd. To make in-range doubles non-additive it must bend in range. It then has to treat a single that reads 0.5 as truly at −570 kcal/mol, because below a reading of about 1 the curve is flat. In simulation the same fit stays sensible with saturation alone (−0.5). It turns absurd in the same way as soon as nonspecific epistasis is present (−2.3 to −2.8).

Verdict. A readout alone is not a consistent explanation. A link (the model's readout curve) that is asked to absorb nonspecific epistasis will degenerate.

The shape of the nonspecific term

The simulations model nonspecific epistasis as a step, not a Gaussian hump. It is +0.45 kcal/mol for doubles whose additive latent dG is below about 1.3, and it fades to 0 above about 2.7: a logistic curve centred at 2 with width 0.35 (Figure 3e, dashed line). Its parameters were set by eye to match the in-range remainder of Test 3. They were not fitted.

It looks like a hump on the measured scale because the readout is flat below the floor. Whatever the protein does to a double that is unfolded anyway, the assay reads the floor. What reaches the measurement is roughly the readout's local slope times the term (Figure S1, preset "nonspecific below the floor"). The rising left side of the hump is therefore the readout's slope, not a property of the protein. Below x ≈ −0.5 the data say nothing about nonspecific epistasis at all.

After Test 2, the in-range data fit a tilt as well as they fit a step: dddG falls by about 0.6 to 0.7 kcal/mol between x ≈ 0.5 and 3, and crosses zero somewhere between x ≈ 1.5 and above 3, depending on whose wild-type reference is right. The training term in the queue, --nonspecific_term fx, is piecewise linear with a free value every 0.5 kcal/mol, so it assumes neither shape.

What is settled, and what is not

  • Settled. In range (x above about 0.5), dddG falls with x by about 0.6 to 0.7 kcal/mol in both readouts. This holds even when every variant reads far above the floor and the wild type far below the ceiling. It is not saturation. It is nonspecific epistasis, or some other effect that both readouts share.
  • Not settled.
    • Its level, uncertain by about ±0.2 kcal/mol (Test 2).
    • Its size and shape at the knee, −1 < x < 0, where saturation and nonspecific epistasis trade off and the lift test disagrees with the simple step (Test 4).
    • Anything below the floor, which the assay cannot see.
  • For training. f(x̂) is identifiable in range. At the knee it will trade off against the link's floor.

Nonspecific epistasis beyond x

Smoothers fitted on the 156 non-validation libraries and scored on the validation doubles: E[dddG | x] explains 56% of the variance of dddG, a surface in the two single effects 64% (dG_wt adds nothing beyond x). The extra 8 points come almost entirely out of the pair level (Figure 2). At matched x, doubles of two very unequal singles fall 0.27 kcal/mol below the curve. Among doubles whose singles are both well inside the range (dG ≥ 1.5) the deficit disappears (+0.19 against the pooled curve for |ddG_A − ddG_B| below 0.5, +0.18 at 1 to 2, +0.01 above 2 on 374 doubles), while doubles with one compressed single (dG < 0.5) carry it (−0.12 to −0.35). The beyond-x part is therefore mostly the readout: a compressed single makes x overstate the double. It is not a separate biophysical effect, but it reaches the pair and line levels all the same.

Where they matter

  • Doubles below the floor (x < −1; 18% of validation doubles, 34% of the sum of squares of dddG). Readout saturation: the double sits near its library's practical floor (median dG +0.29 in validation), so dddG ≈ floor − x, a deterministic function of the singles. A saturation curve built from the singles reproduces this region. These doubles carry almost no information about which residues interact, except rescue: a double measured well above the floor despite x below it.
  • The knee and the lower range (−1 < x < 1.7, about 60% of doubles). Both. The readout compresses near-floor singles (from dG ≈ 1 down) and doubles; the nonspecific hump adds +0.3 to +0.5 kcal/mol throughout and is most of the trend in range.
  • Any statistic on raw dddG, at any level. On the observed scale h(c+a+b) is not additive in a and b, so saturation survives centring by pair, by line and even double-centring. The old raw metrics gave a saturation-only control 0.50 (pair), 0.53 (line) and 0.26 (double-centred cell) on real validation data.
  • The pair and line levels, even on residuals. Nonspecific epistasis that depends on the two effects separately, and the compressed singles behind most of it, are not functions of x; neither are wild-type reference errors. They inflate the pair level most (Table 7).
  • Ranking dddG (not ddG_AB) inside a column. dddG subtracts ddG_A, which varies along a column, so its order is not monotone-invariant.

Where they do not

  • The order of doubles inside one column. Same library, same partner, one monotone h: the order of ddG_AB is the order of the latent stabilities. A nonspecific term that is monotone in each single effect (the hump, the product form) leaves it unchanged too. The cell-rank metric is built on it and scores 0 for every nonspecific form simulated.
  • Residuals beyond each side's own global curve. Subtracting E[dG_AB | x] from the measurement, and each head's own additive prediction (through its own link) from the prediction, removes the saturation term and every nonspecific term that is a function of x, exactly. The magnitude metrics are built on it. Nonspecific terms beyond x need the two-stage device (The metric set).

What the global level is. About three quarters of it is the readout: a floor near 0 that varies by library and by protease and compresses singles as well as doubles. About a quarter is nonspecific epistasis: an in-range decline of about 0.6 to 0.7 kcal/mol across x, shared by both readouts and so a property of the stability (or of the dG inference both readouts share); +0.3 to +0.5 kcal/mol in the combined readout, a level uncertain by about ±0.2. A monotone link can model the first part only; forced to fit both, it degenerates. Every model so far leaves most of the hump unexplained (global bias −0.5 to −0.7 kcal/mol), and the MT head carries part of what it does explain as latent epistasis (its bias is smaller than the combined head's). Binned by prediction, the released objective without a link is miscalibrated near the floor (singles it predicts at dG −0.92 are measured at +0.14 on average); with the link, at epoch 2, only 53 validation singles are predicted below −0.5 (525 without), and the remaining gap of about +0.4 near predicted dG 0 tracks a floor still too low.

The raw material

For each validation double the data give:

  • c, the wild type's stability (dG_wt);
  • ddG_A and ddG_B, how much each single substitution changes it (negative = destabilising);
  • ddG_AB, how much the double changes it.

Two quantities are derived from these:

  • dddG = ddG_AB − ddG_A − ddG_B: how far the double is from the sum of its parts. Positive means the double is more stable than its two singles add up to.
  • The additive expectation x = c + ddG_A + ddG_B: the stability the double would have if the two substitutions simply added up.

The model predicts a ddG for every single and double, so it has its own version of each of these numbers.

All the doubles at one pair of positions (i, j) form a grid. Each row is one substitution at i, each column one substitution at j, and each cell is the double that combines them. Validation has 24 such grids from 9 proteins, each about 18 × 18 to 19 × 19: 8,494 doubles that have both singles measured.

Four tools do all the work. Each is shown on its own first, then all four together on one grid.

Tool 1. The global curve: averaging along x

Sort the doubles by x, cut them into 20 groups of equal size, and take each group's mean dddG. Joined up, these means form a curve: the typical dddG of a double with a given x, written E[dddG | x] ("the expected dddG given x").

  • Anything that depends only on how destabilised a double is ends up in this curve. That includes saturation, and nonspecific epistasis that is a function of x.
  • Anything that depends on which residues are involved averages out of it.

Subtracting the curve from each double's dddG leaves its residual: how much more or less non-additive this double is than a typical double with the same x.

Figure M1 builds a synthetic validation set: 2,400 doubles from 60 made-up proteins, each protein with its own floor, and noise of 0.2 kcal/mol per reading. It compares the measured curve with four predictions. The two global metrics summarise the gap between the curves in each group.

Tool 2. Spearman correlation: do two lists agree in order?

Most of the metrics are Spearman correlations. To compute one:

  1. Replace each number in the first list by its rank: 1 for the smallest, 2 for the next, and so on.
  2. Do the same for the second list.
  3. Measure how well the two rankings agree: +1 when the order is identical, 0 when there is no relation, −1 when it is exactly reversed.

Spearman looks only at order. It ignores units and scale, and any curve that always rises (a monotone curve, like the readout) leaves it unchanged. Pearson correlation, the usual kind, measures agreement in the values themselves, so it does change. In Figure M2, add noise to a prediction and watch both fall. Then squash the prediction through a floor and watch only Pearson move.

Tool 3. Double-centring: what is left after rows and columns

Take a grid of numbers. Subtract each row's mean, then subtract each column's mean (of what is left). The result is the part of each cell that cannot be written as "this row is high" plus "this column is high". A grid that is purely a row effect plus a column effect becomes exactly zero.

  • In a double-mutant grid, a row effect means "this substitution at i is generally more (or less) compatible with changes at j, whatever they are".
  • The double-centred remainder means "this particular pair of residues".

Step through Figure M3. Watch what happens to one large interaction: it survives, slightly reduced, and leaves a faint negative trace along its row and column, because it had raised their means.

Tool 4. Ranks inside one column

All the doubles in one column share the same partner substitution and the same protein, so they all pass through the same readout curve. A curve that only rises can squash values together, but it can never swap two of them. So the order of the doubles' measured ddG_AB down a column is the order of their true stabilities, however much the floor compresses them. Replacing each column by its ranks gives numbers the floor cannot touch.

A nonspecific term that grows steadily with each single's effect behaves the same way. Down one column the partner is fixed, so on its own such a term can stretch the column but cannot reorder it. A prediction that knows only such a term therefore scores exactly 0 on the rank metric. (Combined with specific effects in the measured data, it can occasionally shift a close pair, which only adds noise.) Measurement noise can also swap two doubles, and does so more easily where the floor has squashed them close together.

Question

How

Fooled by
Nothing residue-specific can earn it. It rewards a good readout curve and a nonspecific term.

Judges the link, floor handling and any nonspecific term.

Question

How
The same gaps as global_err, averaged with their signs.
Values

Question

line_rho

Question
Does the prediction know which substitutions are generally more, or less, compatible with changes at the other position?
How

Fooled by
Nothing earns it falsely, but measurement noise lowers it for every model. dddG subtracts a single's measured value from every cell of that single's line, so the single's measurement error becomes a line effect that no model can predict.

cell_mag

Question

How

One of the three parts of the cell score.

cell_rank

Question
Does the prediction know when the partner changes which substitution is more stable?
How
In each complete block:
  1. Replace each column of measured ddG_AB by its ranks (Tool 4). Do the same for the prediction's latent ddG_AB.
  2. Double-centre both rank grids (Tool 3) and compute the Spearman correlation.
  3. Repeat with rows in place of columns, and average the two.
Finally, average over grids.
Values

Fooled by
Nothing tested. It is immune to saturation (a column's order survives the readout) and to every nonspecific form simulated.
Use
The stopping and checkpoint metric (cell_rank_mt).

cell_flipacc

Question
When two substitutions swap order depending on the partner, does the prediction get the direction right?
How
Take two rows (i, i′) and two columns (j, j′) of one grid. It is a confident reversal when i is more stable than i′ by more than 0.6 kcal/mol with partner j, and less stable by more than 0.6 with partner j′. The prediction's contrast (L_ij − L_i′j) − (L_ij′ − L_i′j′) says how much more i beats i′ with j than with j′. It is right when it has the same sign as the measured swap (Figure M5). Values
0.5 is a coin toss. An additive prediction's contrast is exactly 0, because the single effects cancel; it is counted as half right, giving 0.5. Trained models score 0.80 to 0.88.
Fooled by
Product-form nonspecific epistasis (0.57). Slightly by saturation (0.54), because noise swaps are commoner in columns the floor has compressed.
Use
One of the three parts of the cell score. Read it as secondary.

A diagnostic. If cell_sigsd rises along with cell_rank, the model expresses more interaction; that is not necessarily more correct interaction.

Why an additive prediction scores exactly zero

Saturation makes even an additive model's dddG non-zero (Figure S1). If the metrics compared measured residuals with the prediction's raw dddG, a model with a good readout curve would earn credit for saturation alone. So the two sides are built in parallel, each measured against its own curve (Figure M6):

  • Measured side: dddG minus the measured global curve E[dddG | x].
  • Predicted side: the prediction for the double, minus what the prediction's own singles add up to, passed through the prediction's own readout curve. For an additive model this difference is exactly 0 for every double, whatever its readout curve does. Then the prediction's own global curve, averaged along its own predicted x, is removed from what remains.

One correction makes this exact for real models. A model may predict all doubles a little too high or too low by a constant (a calibration bias). Passed through a bent readout curve, that constant would become a pattern that varies with x. Subtracting the model's typical (median) difference before the curve removes it.

The measured side still contains the part of saturation that depends on the two singles separately, such as a compressed single (Test 4). No prediction's residual contains it, so no model can earn it. It only lowers every model's best possible score at the residual levels.

Nonspecific epistasis and the metrics

Figure M7 summarises which sources of non-additivity each metric can credit. The two kinds of nonspecific epistasis behave differently.

  • A function of x, such as the in-range decline of Telling them apart. Both sides remove their global curves, so it vanishes from every residual and rank metric. In simulation, a prediction that knows only this term scores exactly 0 at every residual level and on cell_rank, and 0.48 on reversal accuracy (chance). Only global_err and global_bias credit it: 0.25 → 0.15 and −0.20 → −0.01.
  • A function of the two singles separately, for example a term that grows with the product of the two effects. Two doubles with the same x can then differ: two medium substitutions behave differently from one large and one tiny. So the global curve does not remove the term. Across a grid it is also not a sum of a row part and a column part, so some of it survives into the residual levels. A prediction that knows only such a term earns pair_rho 0.31, beyond_rho 0.16 and reversal accuracy 0.57. cell_rank stays at 0: down one column the partner is fixed, so the term grows steadily with the row's effect and cannot reorder the column.

The two-stage device removes the second kind (Figure M8). After each side's residual, it fits a smooth surface that predicts the residual from three numbers only: the smaller single effect, the larger single effect, and x. Each validation protein is held out in turn and its surface is fitted on the other proteins, so the surface learns only what is common to all proteins, which is what "nonspecific" means. Subtracting it leaves what cannot be predicted from the sizes of the two substitutions.

  • In simulation it brings the product-only prediction to 0 at every level (pair 0.31 → 0.00, beyond 0.16 → −0.01). It keeps the credit for specific effects (pair 0.60 → 0.70, line 0.45 → 0.45, cell_mag 0.27 → 0.26).
  • On the real models it lowers pair_rho by 0.08 to 0.14 and line_rho by up to 0.06, and leaves beyond_rho and cell_mag about where they were (Table 7). About a fifth of the pair-level credit was nonspecific.

Training objectives and experiments that follow

A confident-reversal loss

Each confident reversal in the training matrices fixes the sign of the latent interaction contrast of four doubles, and nothing else. Pair, line and single effects cancel, the measured side is immune to saturation, and the 0.6 kcal/mol margin keeps noise reversals rare. A logistic loss on the MT pass's latent contrast is therefore an interaction-only objective that uses only the reliable part of the signal:

L_flip = mean over confident reversals (i, i′, j, j′) of softplus( −κ · s · (L_ij − L_i′j − L_ij′ + L_i′j′) ), s = sign(y_ij − y_i′j)

The training matrices hold 27,259 such reversals (0.4% of 6.4 million 2 × 2 sub-blocks; median 30 per oriented matrix). The current sampler puts three columns of a pair in a micro-batch, which holds about 1.4 reversals on average (30% of units hold any). The loss would need either whole matrices per step or a detached memory of the other columns' latent predictions to form the contrasts. Zero-shot, the base model gets 87% of the reversals larger than 1 kcal/mol right and 72% of all those larger than 0.6; trained models reach 93 to 96% and 80 to 88%. Compared with the current MT terms, this loss does not reward the single-effect consensus (the within-column ListMLE does) and does not regress on a target at the noise floor (the interaction component of the MT regression does).

Outcome (2026-10-13). Implemented as --lambda_mt_flip, with whole pair matrices per batch (--pack_pair_matrices) so the contrasts can be formed. It did not help: at MT rank 8 the packed arms with weight 0.3, 1 and 3 trailed the unpacked rank-8 arms by 0.01 to 0.05 on cell_rank_mt at epochs 3 to 5, and weight 1 without packing lost 0.07. Reversals also carry nonspecific structure of the product kind (Table 6: a predictor that knows only a product-form nonspecific term reaches 0.57 reversal accuracy), so part of what the loss rewards is not residue-specific.

Saturation and nonspecific epistasis in the objective

The analysis above separates three jobs the current objectives give to one link or to the MT head.

  1. The link should model the readout only, and learn its floor freely. Regressing measured doubles through it places the floor near −0.5 within a few hundred steps. The censoring hinge, as built, works against this: it bounds the observed prediction of a dead variant at −1, which a link whose floor is higher can never meet, so dead variants drag the link down instead of pushing their own latents down. The bound belongs on the latent scale: --censor_hinge_scale latent (devel's default since 2026-10-13; pass observed to reproduce earlier runs) takes it through the link's inverse, so a dead variant must sit at or below the floor's knee and the link gets no gradient from censored items. A per-library or per-protease floor offset is a possible refinement (floors vary by ±0.27).
  2. Teacher forcing should use latent singles. The released objective forces the WT half of a double with its measured singles; near the floor those readings are compressed (the lift test), so the forced additive part is too stable and the MT head compensates. --tf_singles latent_link maps them back through the link, at most 0.5 kcal/mol below the measurement; --tf_singles predicted uses the model's own latent singles instead (claude/main-baseline).
  3. Nonspecific epistasis needs its own term. A link fitted to it degenerates; left to the MT head, it spends capacity on a pattern that is the same for every pair of destabilising substitutions. --nonspecific_term fx (claude/main-baseline) adds a learned piecewise-linear f(x̂) to every multi-mutant's latent before the link, shared by all proteins, with x̂ the additive dG the forcing uses. Simulation on the real designs (Table 8) compares four output structures fitted to singles and doubles from realistic, shrunk single-mutant predictions. A function of the predicted additive dG, f(x̂), recovers about two thirds of the hump's bias (global bias −0.49 → −0.16; curve RMSE 0.54 → 0.26) and does no harm without one. A function of the two singles separately does no better, even when the truth is a product of the two (its extra freedom is spent on noise in the single predictions). A flexible monotone link barely helps (−0.45). None of them changes the singles' error, because the term acts on multi-mutants only. The link-only structure in the hump world reproduces the real models' global bias (−0.49 against −0.5 to −0.7), which supports reading that bias as nonspecific epistasis.
  4. Measure it with the two-stage device. A nonspecific term should lower global_err and move global_bias toward 0 while leaving the two-stage residual levels and cell_rank where they were; if it raises shipped pair_rho but not the two-stage version, it has learned nonspecific structure, not pair-specific coupling.

Table 8. Output structures fitted on a simulated training draw (real training design) and scored on a fresh validation draw (real validation design), one draw per world. Single-mutant predictions: truth plus noise (SD 0.6), shrunk toward the mean as a fitted model's are. Link: one soft floor with learned floor and softness. f(x̂): piecewise linear in the predicted additive dG. f(â, b̂): a small MLP of the two predicted singles and dG_wt. Flexible link: a monotone piecewise-linear h with 14 knots and no f. Global curve: mean dddG in 20 bins of the measured x.

Experiments, in queue order

One GPU, one arm at a time. The first three and the last are the basis recipe (seed 1, 8 epochs) plus the link at micro-batch 64 on claude/main-baseline, about 4.5 h each; each changes one thing and is compared with bl_w2a4_m16a16_capped_link.

  1. Teacher forcing with latent singles (bl_w2a4_m16a16_capped_link_tflatent, running): the measured singles mapped back through the link. Does removing the compression from the forced half lower the global bias and help the cell level?
  2. Teacher forcing with predicted latent singles (…_tfpred): the model's own latent singles, no single label needed.
  3. An explicit nonspecific term f(x̂) (…_link_ns): judge on global_err, global_bias and the two-stage residual levels; the cell level should not change.
  4. Devel's hinge on the latent scale (o6s38_mt8_hinge_latent, devel): o6s09_mt8's flags, 10 epochs, with --censor_hinge_scale latent. The link's floor (link/lo) should stop drifting below −1; compare with o6s09_mt8 epoch by epoch.
  5. Regression weight ×5 with the link (…_reg5, last): heavier regression strengthened the magnitude levels in devel (o6g1_bal111, o6s03_m1). Its flags are to be revised with what the first four show (the forcing mode, the nonspecific term) before it starts.

Each of these should be judged on the cell score with the guards. Run two seeds before believing a difference below 0.03 on cell_rank_mt.

Every validation writes training_logs/<run>/0/val_dump_<tag>.npz with all items' latent scores, the reverse leg, dG_wt and the link. Any dump, past runs included, can be rescored with the current definitions and pair-bootstrap intervals:

PYTHONPATH=src python scripts/epi_from_dump.py training_logs/<run>/0/val_dump_e5.npz [...] --boot 200 --csv out.csv

Absolute values carry the sampling uncertainty of a 24-matrix validation set (the 90% pair-bootstrap interval of cell_rank_mt for the baseline is 0.14 to 0.27); paired comparisons on the same set are an order of magnitude tighter (Table 5).
